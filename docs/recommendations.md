# Recommendations

Detailed findings and the actions they support. Delivery numbers come from real Olist orders (2016 to 2018). Anything marked **simulated** comes from the synthetic inventory layer and shows how the method would work, not what Olist sellers actually held. This file is generated by `python -m sca.recommend`, so every figure traces back to the marts.

## 1. Late delivery is the most expensive operational problem in the data

6,534 of 96,470 measurable orders (6.8%) arrived after the promised date. On time orders average a review score of 4.29. Late orders average 2.27, and 62% of them get one or two stars, against 9% of on time orders. Late orders also carry 986K BRL of the 13.6M BRL order value (7.3%), which is the revenue most exposed to a lost repeat customer.

## 2. The problem is carrier transit, not the delivery promise

| Days | On time orders | Late orders |
|---|---:|---:|
| Purchase to carrier pickup (seller side) | 3.0 | 6.1 |
| Carrier pickup to delivery (carrier side) | 8.0 | 27.9 |
| Delivery promised at purchase | 23.8 | 22.6 |

Late orders were promised about the same window as on time orders, so a short promise is not the cause. They take 23 more days in total, and 87% of that extra time is spent in carrier transit. Across all orders the average promise is roughly twice the actual delivery time, so estimates are already padded heavily.

**Action:** do not widen estimates further. Focus on the slow tail of carrier transit and set carrier service levels on the 95th percentile, not the average.

## 3. Slow handoff to the carrier is a usable early warning

| Time from purchase to carrier pickup | Orders | Late rate | Avg review |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Up to 3 days | 59,397 | 4.7% | 4.25 |
| 3 to 5 days | 19,999 | 6.7% | 4.13 |
| 5 to 8 days | 11,043 | 9.2% | 4.02 |
| More than 8 days | 5,894 | 23.3% | 3.53 |

Orders that wait more than 8 days for pickup are 5.0 times more likely to be late than orders picked up within 3 days. That is known days before the customer sees a problem.

**Action:** flag every order that reaches day 5 without a carrier scan, escalate to the seller, and offer expedited shipping or proactive customer contact at day 8. As an illustration, if that intervention halved the late rate for the 5,894 orders above 8 days, it would prevent about 686 late deliveries, or 11% of all late orders. The halving is an assumption to be tested, not a measured result.

## 4. Concentrate on five states and a small group of sellers

**Customer states with the lowest on time rate (at least 300 orders):**

These five Northeast states are 3.3% of orders but 7.7% of late orders. Bringing them to the national rate would remove about 286 late deliveries. A slow tail of 38 to 46 days at the 95th percentile points to long haul carrier legs rather than a seller problem.

On the seller side, 78 of 425 sellers with at least 50 orders run a late rate above 10%. They ship 11% of orders but cause 21% of late deliveries, about twice their share.

## 6. Fix reorder points and release excess stock **(simulated)**

Across 1,893 product and distribution centre combinations, 53 are stocked out or at risk today, 494 hold more than 120 days of supply (116K of 458K BRL, 25% of inventory at cost), and 1,026 sold nothing in the last 13 weeks. Demand is a long tail, so most product and region pairs sell only occasionally.

The baseline policy plans with each supplier's quoted lead time. I replayed the 400 series with any stockout weeks using the same demand and the same lead time draws, but with reorder points built on the observed 90th percentile lead time:

| Measure | Current policy | Revised policy |
|---|---:|---:|
| Reorder points raised | n/a | 121 of 400 series |
| Average fill rate | 88.2% | 90.4% |
| Share of weeks in stockout | 3.3% | 2.5% |
| Average inventory value (BRL) | 126,439 | 137,568 (+8.8%) |

**Action:** raise reorder points on the series flagged above and accept the extra inventory only where a supplier is unreliable. Review the long tail of slow movers for central stocking in one distribution centre, and release the overstock before placing new orders.

## Caveats

Delivery findings come from 2016 to 2018 Brazilian marketplace data and show the method, not current performance. Inventory and purchase order figures come from a seeded simulation described in `docs/assumptions.md`, so treat impact estimates as directional. Review scores are a customer reported signal and are only linked to delivery by association, not proven cause. See the README for the full list of assumptions.
